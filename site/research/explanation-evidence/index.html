Explanation

When a clear explanation becomes evidence.

Asking a learner to make an idea teachable does not just consolidate what they know. It produces the most reliable signal we have of what they do not.

By Mira Mendel Explanation thread Jul 29, 2026 6 min read
Download the report All research

An explanation in draft beside the geometric study it was asked to become — the seam between the two is the finding.

There is a moment in every real explanation when the speaker has to choose what to leave out. That choice is where the gap between understanding and memorising shows up most clearly—and it is also where the next useful question lives.

Key takeaways

  • The "explain it back" prompt is a signal, not a retention booster. Across 1,847 episodes its value was diagnostic: it shows where the idea is still on loan from the previous answer.
  • The seam is findable. In 61% of episodes the first vague sentence — the boundary condition, the hedge, the "and then you just…" — appeared before any probe from the tutor.
  • Short beats long. Learners whose explanations were short but re-shapable scored 74% on a two-week transfer task; long, fluent, stable explanations scored 52%.
  • Three follow-ups shipped. Compression, edge, and transfer prompts now wait for the learner to finish before they appear.

The "explain it back" prompt, three years on

Three years of A/B data on "explain this in your own words" prompts, both inside Socrates and in the broader literature, converge on the same result: the prompt works, but not for the reason most people assume. It does not primarily improve retention. It produces a signal.

The signal is uneven in a predictable way. When a learner can explain an idea cleanly, in their own framing, without lifting the structure of the previous answer, we have a reliable marker that the concept has been assembled. When they cannot—and especially when they cannot at the points where the idea actually has edges—the explanation shows the seam.

What we see in the seam

The most common seam is the boundary condition. A learner will explain a definition with confidence, then hesitate at the first edge case. They will be precise about a formula, then vague about the moment when the formula stops applying. They will describe a method crisply, then reach for "and then you just…" at exactly the place where the method actually requires a judgment.

This pattern is hard to fake, and it is hard to recover from without help. We see it most clearly when the learner has tried to explain to an imagined audience—a friend, a younger student, a future version of themselves. The imagined audience does the work of compressing the idea, and the compression exposes the part that is still on loan from the previous answer.

The point at which a learner's explanation becomes vague is exactly the point at which the next question belongs.

What we tried next

For a year we have asked the tutor to listen for the seam and offer a follow-up question at exactly that point. The result was uneven, and the failure mode was instructive: the tutor sometimes inserted a follow-up too early, before the learner had time to find the gap themselves. The interruption felt like a quiz, not a conversation.

The current behaviour is closer to waiting. The tutor finishes the learner's explanation, marks the seam silently, and then offers one of three prompts: say it back to me in fewer words; give me an example where the idea almost fails; or explain it as if to someone who already knows the basics. All three are small re-framings of the same idea—compression, edge, transfer—and we are still measuring which of them moves retention most.

The thing we have stopped believing

We used to think that a longer explanation was a better explanation. The data no longer supports that. Learners whose explanations run on the shorter side, but who can re-shape the idea on demand, consistently outperform learners whose explanations are long and stable. The first group has the idea. The second group has the previous answer.

This is the working principle behind the current tutor behaviour. We are not optimising for eloquence. We are optimising for the moment when a learner can finish their sentence, look at the result, and know that it is theirs.

How we measured it

We sampled transcripts where a learner had produced at least one full explanation. Three coders marked each explanation for boundary-condition vagueness, hedge phrases, and omitted edge cases; inter-rater agreement was κ = 0.74. A seam was defined as the first sentence where the explanation stopped being specific—typically a "then you just…" or a silent slide past a condition.

Transfer was measured with a delayed, re-worded problem that required applying the idea in a context the learner had not seen. We report the proportion of learners who produced a correct first move within five minutes.

Limitations

Three caveats bound these claims. The episodes come from one tutoring product, so the seam signal may partly reflect our own prompt phrasing. The coders share a team with the prompt designers, despite the blinding protocol. And because the three follow-up prompts shipped together in May, their relative effects are still confounded with the learners who have since adapted to them—a controlled comparison is in progress.

Open questions

We still do not know whether the three follow-up prompts—compression, edge, transfer—work equally across subjects. Compression appears to matter more in proof-heavy subjects; edge cases appear to matter more in empirical ones. We are also studying whether the seam signal degrades once learners learn the tutor's marking behaviour and start performing gaps rather than having them.
